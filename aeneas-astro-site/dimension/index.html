About Me

I'm a recent physics graduate from the University of Connecticut, preparing to apply to graduate programs in astronomy and astrophysics for the Fall 2027 semester. My research focuses on star formation in nearby galaxies, using multi-wavelength survey data to understand how gas converts into stars across different galactic environments.

My long-term goal is to become a research astronomer, and I'm especially drawn to understanding how the kinematics of gas influence the evolution of stars and galaxies. Understanding the affects of stability criterions such as the TOOMRE Q parameter and the Jeans instability when observed under high-resolution pixel-to-pixel analysis could prove useful in understanding the small scale physics of both star and galaxy evolution.

Outside of research, I'm passionate about science education and plan to be involved in a number of outreach programs and tutoring opportunities in the near future. I am currently working as a lab assistant at TRADEBE, an environmental services company where we test Chemical Oxygen Demand, Polychlorinated Biphenyls, and hazardous materials using Gas Chromatography and Electron Capture Detection.

Senior Research Project

KS Relation in NGC 1566

I worked as part of the PHANGS collaboration with Dr. Chris Faesi during my senior year, examining the Kennicutt 1998 star formation relation — the empirical link between gas surface density and star formation rate — at ~100 pc resolution in the nearby spiral galaxy NGC 1566.

The analysis combines dust-corrected Hα emission from PHANGS–MUSE (tracing recent star formation) with CO(2–1) emission from PHANGS–ALMA (tracing molecular gas), resolved on a pixel-by-pixel basis across different galactic environments including spiral arms, the bar, and disk.

We used a Bayesian hierarchical MCMC regression fitting model (LinMix, Kelly 2007) that accounts for per-pixel measurement errors, making it more statistically robust than an ordinary least-squares fit for this type of astronomical analysis.
